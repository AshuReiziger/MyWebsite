**WHO THIS IS FOR**

Designers and studios who are tired of starting projects on vague instructions and finding out the real requirements three revisions in.

**HOW TO USE IT**

Send this to a client to fill out before kickoff, or fill it out together on a call. Don't start design work until every field has a real answer — "whatever looks good" is not an answer.

## 1. PROJECT BASICS

- **Client / business name**
- **What are we designing? (be specific: logo, flyer, full identity, packaging, app UI...)**
- **Deadline — and is it a real deadline (event, print date) or a soft one?**
- **Budget range agreed**

## 2. THE REAL OBJECTIVE

This is the section most briefs skip, and the one that saves the most revisions.

- **What should this design accomplish? (e.g. "get more walk-ins," "look credible to investors," "stand out on a crowded shelf")**
- **How will you know it worked, after it's out in the world?**

## 3. AUDIENCE

- **Who is this actually for? Describe one real person, not a demographic label.**
- **What do they already think or feel about this brand, if anything?**

## 4. DIRECTION & CONSTRAINTS

- **Brands, competitors, or references the client admires — and why**
- **Anything the client has explicitly ruled out (colors, styles, comparisons to avoid)**
- **Mandatory elements (logo lockup, legal text, tagline, existing brand colors)**

## 5. DELIVERY

- **Where will this be used? (print, web, social, signage, packaging — list all)**
- **Required file formats and sizes**
- **Who has final sign-off? (name the actual decision-maker, not "the team")**

**BEFORE YOU START**

If Section 2 is blank or vague, stop — go back to the client before opening any design software. A brief without a real objective produces revisions, not decisions.

## NEXT STEPS

**A strong brief is the cheapest insurance a design project has. Keep the filled-out version attached to the project file — when a client asks for a change late in the process, it's the fastest way to tell whether the request is a fix or a new project.**
